THEORY 
Resistivity of a metal conductor can be determined from the resistance R of the metal 
conductor and its dimensions. The resistance of a metal conductor is the ability of the 
conductor to inhibit the flow of current passing through it. From Ohm’s law which states 
that when a voltage (potential difference) ∆V is applied across the ends of a conductor, 
the current in the conductor is found to be proportional to the applied voltage. This law 
will hold if the temperature of the conductor is kept constant. 
The resistance of a piece of wire depends on its length, and its cross-sectional area, or 
thickness. The longer the wire is, the greater its resistance. If one wire is twice as long as 
a wire of identical diameter and material, the longer wire offers twice as much resistance 
as the shorter one. A thicker wire, however, has less resistance, because a thick wire 
offers more room for an electric current to pass through than a thin wire does. Scientists 
describe this relationship between resistance, length, and area by saying that resistance is 
proportional to length and inversely proportional to cross-sectional area. That is:

PROCEDURE 
The lengths of the connecting cords provided were measured and recorded. Using the connection 
box, one of the connecting cords provided were connected in series with the powers supply and 
the ammeter. The input of the amplifier was connected to the sockets of the connecting cord 
connector and the voltmeter was connected to the output of the amplifier. The voltage source was 
set to 12V and the current was varied at constant intervals of 0.2A.

The voltage drop across the cord was measured and recorded for each current selected. The 
experiment was repeated for the two remaining connecting cords provided. On the same graph, 
voltage V against current I was plotted for each of the cords. The slope for each graph was 
determined.
