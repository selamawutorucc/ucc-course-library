KINETIC THEORY AND HEAT 
 
The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics 
The zeroth law of thermodynamics states that if two systems are both in thermal equilibrium 
with a third system, then the first two systems are also in thermal equilibrium with each other. 
 
In simple terms the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics states that systems that are in thermal 
equilibrium exist at the same temperature. Figure 1 shows three systems in thermal equilibrium. 
Let A, B, and C be three systems. If A and C are in thermal equilibrium, and A and B are in thermal 
equilibrium, then B and C are in thermal equilibrium. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Figure 1.   The double arrow represents thermal equilibrium between systems. If systems A and 
C are in equilibrium, and systems A and B are in equilibrium, then systems B and C are in 
equilibrium. The systems A, B, and C are at the same temperature. 
 
Systems are in thermal equilibrium if they do not transfer heat, even though they are in a position 
to do so, based on other factors. For example, food that is stored in a refrigerator overnight is in 
thermal equilibrium with the air in the refrigerator: heat no longer flows from one source (the food) 
to the other source (the air) or back. 
 
Temperature and Temperature Scales 
The temperature of a substance is a number which expresses its degree of hotness on some chosen 
scale. Thus, the temperature of water fetched from the tap is lower than that of a boiling water; the 
boiling water contains a much larger quantity of internal energy than the tap water. Temperature 
is measured by means of a thermometer, of which a number of different types are available, but 
each makes use of a particular thermometric property of a particular thermometric substance. Some 
depend on the uniform expansion of a liquid when heated; others on the linear uniform expansion 
of a compound strip of two metals. In order to establish a temperature scale it is necessary to make 
use of fixed points: A fixed point is the single temperature at which it can confidently be expected 
that a particular physical event (e.g. the melting of ice under specific conditions) always takes 
place. Three such points are defined below. 
1. The upper fixed point or steam point is the temperature of steam from water boiling under 
standard atmospheric pressure of 760 mmHg . It is labelled 100 oC or 373 K. 
2. The lower fixed point or ice point is the temperature of pure melting ice. It is labelled 0 oC 
or 273 K. 
3. The triple point of water is that unique temperature at which pure ice, pure water and water 
vapour can exist together in equilibrium. The triple point of water is 273.16 K, or 0.01 °C, or 
32.018 °F.

Thermometers 
A thermometer is a device that measures temperature or a temperature gradient. A thermometer 
has two important elements:  
1. A temperature sensor. In liquid-in-glass thermometers, the bulb of thermometric liquid is 
the sensor in which a change occurs when there is change in temperature.   
2. A means of converting this change into numerical value on a visible scale.  
 
Thermometric Materials 
Certain substances have the property that changes with temperature. Substances that show a 
uniform change in temperature can be used as a thermometric material. For example, some 
substances expand on heating and contract on cooling, some change their colours and some also 
change their electric resistance. Common liquid-in-glass thermometers are generally made using 
some suitable liquid like mercury or alcohol as thermometric material.  
 
A thermometric liquid should have the following properties: 
i. 
It should be visible. 
ii. 
It should have uniform thermal expansion. 
iii. 
It should have a low freezing point. 
iv. 
It should have a high boiling point. 
v. 
It should not wet glass. 
vi. 
It should be a good conductor of heat. 
vii. 
It should have a small specific heat capacity 
viii. 
It should respond to temperature change rapidly

Calibration of Thermometers 
Thermometers can be calibrated either by comparing them with other calibrated thermometers or 
by checking them against known fixed points on the temperature scale. The best known of these 
fixed points are the melting and boiling points of pure water. (Note that the boiling point of water 
varies with pressure, so this must be controlled.) 
The traditional way of putting a scale on a liquid-in-glass or liquid-in-metal thermometer was in 
three stages: 
1. Immerse the sensing portion in a stirred mixture of pure ice and water at atmospheric 
pressure and mark the point indicated when it had come to thermal equilibrium. 
2. Immerse the sensing portion in a steam bath at Standard atmospheric pressure and again 
mark the point indicated. 
3. Divide the distance between these marks into equal portions according to the temperature 
scale being used.

Types of Thermometers 
A liquid in glass thermometer has a bulb with a long capillary tube of uniform and fine bore. A 
suitable liquid is filled in the bulb. When the bulb contacts a hot object, the liquid in it expands 
and rises in the tube. The glass stem of a thermometer is thick and acts as a cylinder lens. This 
makes it easy to see the liquid level in the glass tube. Mostly Mercury and alcohol are used in 
glass thermometers. There are many types of thermometers, but some common thermometers are 
laboratory thermometer, clinical thermometer, thermocouple, Bimetallic strip thermometer and 
Pyrometers. 
Laboratory Thermometer 
It is used to measure the temperature of solids, liquids and gases in experiments, as well as the 
temperature of the environment. It measures temperature in the range of -5 °C to 110 °C. 
 
 
 
Clinical Thermometer 
It is used to measure human body temperatures.

Heat Energy  
Heat is a measure of the total kinetic energy of the atoms or molecules in a body. Heat is a form 
of energy measured in Joules (J). The heat content of a body will depend on its temperature, its 
mass, and the material it is made of. Heat energy is always transferred from an object at high 
temperature to one at lower temperature. 
 
Internal energy is all of the energy belonging to a system while it is stationary. Thermal energy is 
the portion of internal energy that changes when the temperature of a system changes. 
In physics, heat is defined as the energy which is transferred from one place to another owing to a 
temperature difference between them. When you heat a substance, you are transferring energy into

very hot. 
 
Heat Capacity and Specific Heat Capacity 
When heat energy is transferred to a body, its temperature rises but the state remains unchanged. 
The quantity of heat energy required to raise the temperature of a given mass of a substance by 
one degree Celsius varies from one substance to another.

is referred to as the Heat Capacity of that substance. For example, if the initial temperature of a 
metal rod is 26 0C, then the heat energy required to raise its temperature to 27 0C is its Heat 
Capacity. Heat capacity is denoted by C and its SI unit is J/K.

Specific Heat Capacity  
We know that when heat energy is absorbed by a substance, its temperature increases. If the same 
quantity of heat is given to equal masses of different substances, it is observed that the rise in 
temperature for each substance is different. This is due to the fact that different substances have 
different heat capacities. For example, the quantity of energy needed to raise the temperature of 
one 1 kg of water by 1 0C is 4186 J, but the quantity of energy needed to raise the temperature of 
1 kg of copper by 1 0C is only 387 J. 
Specific heat capacity (c) is defined as the quantity of heat energy required to raise the temperature 
of 1 Kg of a substance by 10 C.

Latent Heat 
Another important effect of heat is that it can produce a change in a body’s state. As we know 
matter can exist in any of the three states- solid, liquid or gas. A change of matter from one physical 
state to another is called change of state. The state of matter depends on the temperature and the 
pressure that is exerted on it. The quantity of heat energy required to change the temperature of 
matter but not necessarily changing its state is called Sensible Heat. There are other cases where 
heat energy absorbed by or removed from matter does not change its temperature but changes its 
state.  Such energy is referred to as hidden heat or latent heat. This energy is called hidden or latent 
because instead of the heat raising the temperature of the matter, it rather works on the molecules 
breaking them further apart. Latent heat is the quantity of heat required to change completely the 
state of matter to another state without any change in temperature. A typical example is the quantity 
of heat required to completely change 1 kg of ice into water. This process takes place at a constant 
temperature of 00 C. This kind of heat required for this process is called the Latent Heat of Fusion 
(Lf). On the other hand, the quantity of heat required to completely change water into vapour at 
constant temperature of 1000 C is called Latent Heat of Vaporization (Lv).
