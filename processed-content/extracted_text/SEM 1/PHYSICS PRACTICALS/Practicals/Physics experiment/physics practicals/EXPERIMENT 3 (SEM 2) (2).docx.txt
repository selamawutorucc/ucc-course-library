THEORY: The fundamental frequency of a string of length l tensioned at both ends is given by: f = 1/2L x (√T/µ), where T = tension and µ = mass per unit length. This theory basically states that the frequency at which a string vibrates is directly equivalent to one over twice the length of the string multiplied by the squared root of the tension in the string over the squared root of the mass per unit length of the string. Standing waves arise because identical waves (equal frequency, velocity and amplitude) travel on a string in opposite directions in the same medium and combine in accord with the principle of superposition on each other. Consider a string that has length L and is fixed at both ends. The string has numerous natural patterns of vibration known as normal modes and each of these has a characteristic frequency. The normal modes form a harmonic series. The first harmonic with the lowest frequency is called the fundamental note and its frequency is referred to as the fundamental frequency. The second harmonic is also known as the first overtone.

PROCEDURE: The string was laid across two triangular sliders clamped between a fixed hook and a spring balance, as shown above. The string balance was attached to a string tensioned with fishing line. The tensioned force was exactly 10 N. The string length was set by moving the triangular sliders along the measuring scale. The vibration of the string was then optically scanned midway between the sliders. The signal from the photo-cell, fitted with an aperture slit, was amplified and transmitted to the oscilloscope and the counter/timer. The string vibration was to be started with a gentle tap of the rubber hammer, and the counter/timer was to be started once the harmonics had died away. The tension and diameter of the nickel string used were measured and recorded. With the tension constant at 10 N, a string length of 40cm was measured between the two triangular sliders, the counter/timer device was set to record frequency (KHz), the sensor was aligned to the string using light so that the counter read zero, the start button was pushed and then the string was struck gently at the middle, between the two triangular sliders, to start the vibrations. Once the frequency became stable; the harmonics died away, the stop button was pushed and the frequency was recorded. The process was repeated for four other values of the l = 50, 60, 70, and 80 cm. The frequency at these lengths was recorded.

DISCUSSION:
According to the results obtained from the experiment; looking at the first table, with constant tension of 10N in a string, the frequency of vibration decreases as the length of the string increases. That is an inverse proportionality relation. Although the same string was used in this experiment, the change in length of the string had a direct influence on the mass per unit length of the string, as is shown in the calculations above. It can also be observed that the frequency of vibration in the string was directly dependent on the tension in the string and inversely dependent on the mass per unit length of the string. The graph shows a correlation between the frequency of vibration and one by each of the lengths of the string used in the experiment. It clearly shows an inverse relationship between frequency and the length of the string, as the slope of the graph shows. In view of these observations, it can be concluded from the simplified equation of the theory above that theory is correct.
SORCES OF ERROR:
- Errors may have occurred from the measuring instrument, which was moved when the length of the string was being set. This may have caused inaccuracy in length determinations.
- The digital oscilloscope may have given inaccurate readings of the exact frequency of vibration because of inaccuracy in placing the string directly in front of its scanner.
PRECAUTIONS:
- The equipment was reset after every reading.
- The string was struck at the same point each time to ensure accuracy and uniformity in each of the values of frequency taken.
 CONCLUSION:
The aim of the experiment to determine the density; the mass per unit length of the string was achieved, and the theory was proved to be accurate.
