SEQUENCE OF TENSE
This refers to an agreement between the verb phrase in both the subordinate and the main clause. Another name for sequence of tense is Agreement of tense. With sequence of tense, the subordinate clause always follows the tense in the principal clause (main clause). Sequence of tense deals with logic; an action in the future can’t happen before an action in the past. The past must come before the present, and the present before the future. The verb in the subordinate clause is changed according to the tense of the main verb. For instance, when the main clause is in the past tense, the subordinate cause must also be in the past tense. Sequence of tense is governed by a number of rules but there is an exception with one of the rules. Below are the rules governing sequence of tense with examples each.
RULE 1
If the verb in the principal clause is in the present or future tense then the tense in the subordinate clause can be in any tense such as the present continuous, future or past tense depending upon the sense expressed.
EXAMPLES: 
Rose says she likes rice.
He will say he likes music.
She says that she will think about it. 
He says that he is fine.
RULE 2
If the tense in the principal clause is in the past tense, the tense in the subordinate clause will be in the corresponding past tense.
EXAMPLES:
We knew that he would not come.
I found out that she was out of town.
Mary said she liked the idea.
He said he had no money to pay his rent.
However, this rule has an exception.
A past tense in the main clause may be followed by a present tense in the subordinate clause when the subordinate clause expresses a universal truth. This is because a universal truth is a timeless idea and not tied to time.
EXAMPLES:
The past emphasized that God is a spirit.
Even the early doctors knew that washing hands prevents infection.
My pastor told me that honesty is the best policy.
We were thought that the earth moves around the sun.
RULE 3
If the subordinate clause is an adjective clause, it may be in any tense as is required by the tense. An adjective clause normally begins with a relative pronoun (who, whom, that or which). 
EXAMPLES:
Henry met a girl who sells balloons.
Yesterday, we I met the man who broke up with me.
The headmaster will give an award to the student who was first in class.
RULE 4
A subordinate clause that expresses an adverbial clause of place, reason or comparison may be in any tense, according to the tense expressed. 
EXAMPLES:
The man who was preaching to you today was once a drunkard.
Rose did not get married because she got blind.
She didn’t pass the exams because her hand writing was bad. 
RULE 5
When a subordinate clause is introduced by a conjunction of purpose, we use “may” in the subordinate clause when the main clause is in the present tense and “might” in the subordinate clause when the main clause is in the past tense.
EXAMPLE:
We eat that we may live.
Kofi exercise regularly that he may stay health.
I will work hard that I may succeed.
Mary studied hard so that she might pass the exams.
RULE 6
If the principal clause is in the future tense, we do not use the future tense in the subordinate clause beginning with when, until, before, after, etc. 
EXAMPLES:
I shall wait until she comes.
I will visit you before I leave.
I will call you the food is ready.
RULE 7
Expressions such as if, if only, it is time, it is about time, it is a long time, and wish are usually followed by past tenses. 
EXAMPLES:
If only I knew earlier.
I wish I could eat another ice cream.
If only he had not joined the gang.
RULE 8 
The word lest in a subordinate clause must be followed by the auxiliary verb SHOULD whatever the tense of the verb in the main clause. 
EXAMPLE:
Mary works hard lest she should fail.
She will raise early lest she should miss the flight.
RULE 9
When the subordinate clause is introduced by the word since, the main clause is in the present perfect tense and the subordinate clause is in the past tense.
EXAMPLES:
It has been long since I saw my husband.
It has been twenty years since Mary left us.
