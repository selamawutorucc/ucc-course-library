COMPONENTS OF A TEXT/PASSAGE
There are three broad divisions of a text:

Introductory paragraph/First paragraph.

Body paragraph

Concluding paragraph/Summary


--- SLIDE 4 ---
THINGS TO LOOK OUT FOR IN A PARAGRAPH
A topic sentence (it is the controlling idea of a paragraph).
Supporting sentences (other sentences used to explain the topic sentence).
Each body paragraph in a text with a topic sentence can be termed as a major division.
Each body paragraph in a text with supporting sentences can be used as minor divisions.
Last paragraph is the concluding paragraph/summary.

--- SLIDE 5 ---
OUTLINE
It is defined as a detailed formal statement of a spoken or written text.
It is also a tool used to organize a written composition.
All relevant points in the text are noted in the outline.
RULES FOR OUTLINE
Heading/Title.

Labelling of divisions. (Major and minor).

Indentation. (Leaving space).

SUMMARY
Summary means to condense a lengthy piece of information into a precise form.

A summary provides relevant ideas in a text.

It is a reduction technique for making notes.

It is used to take notes.

Pay attention to the summary task.
Read the text thoroughly.
Provide a suitable title.
Outline the text.
Identify the topic sentences and supporting sentences.
Avoid unnecessary details.
Draft your summary by putting together your outline.

THE COMMA (,)
A) Use a comma to seperate two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or nor, for, yet, so). Examples:
The mini-bus robber had an easy job, for the windows were open.
The students who are in the Red House scored higher marks in the first test, and the Gold House students did better in the second.
Note: Do not use comma between independent clauses not joined by a coordinating conjuction (use a semi-colon instead (;). Example:
He sprained his arm; his temper was  ruined.
Note: Do not use a comma between very short independent clauses. Eg: 
He sings, and she dances (incorrect).
He sings and she dances. (correct).



--- SLIDE 33 ---
THE COMMA (,)
B) Use a comma to seperate words, phrases, or clauses in a series of three or more: 
Words: Mary went to the Kaneshie market and bought items: onions, tomatoes, pepper, beef, and oil.
Phrases: The fashion magazine is available in bookshops, in supermarkets, at news-stands, and on social media.
Clauses: Akweley read intensively, she selected the right exam questions, she planned her exams well, and she worked within the given time frame.




--- SLIDE 34 ---
THE COMMA (,)
C) The comma is used to set off a long prepositional phrase or an introductory subordinate adverbial clause from the main clause of a sentence. Examples:
In the drought year of 1920, many Africans left the continent for Europe. (Preposition clause).
If you had studied very hard at the university, you would have made a good class. (Adverbial clause).
Note: There would be no need for a comma when the subordinate clause follows the main clause Example: ‘You would have made a good class if you had studied very hard at the university.’





--- SLIDE 35 ---
THE COMMA (,)
D) The comma is used after a non-finite or verbless clause at the initial part of a sentence. Examples:
Broken down in the middle of the road, my vehicle was pushed to the pavement by two students (non-finite clause).
When in the army, John learnt how to defend himself in a physical fight (verbless clause).
Note: A non-finite does not have a subject and its verb does not mark tense. Note a verbless clause has no verb in it.

THE SEMI-COLON (;)
It signals a greater break in thought than the comma. It is, however, closer to a period/full stop than to a comma in most of its uses and is often interchangeable with the period.
A) Use a semi-colon to seperate closely related independent clauses that are not joined by a coordinating conjuction:
One of the twins has traveled all over the world; the other rarely leaves home.
His mother won’t let him; she is afarid he might get hurt. 
Note: The semi-colon is particularly effective for showing balance or contrast between two independent clauses. For example:
The forest abound with wildlife; the rivers teem with fish.
Many people are willing to give advice; they are much less inclined to take it.



--- SLIDE 38 ---
THE SEMI-COLON (;)
B) Use a semi-colon between independent clauses when the second clause begins with a transitional expression (conjunctive adverb) such as therefore, however, nevertheless, thus, moreover, also, besides, consequently, meanwhile, otherwise, then, furthermore, likewise, in fact, still, hence, in addition, for example, etc.
Note: A comma comes in front of the transitional connective. The following are examples:
The gadget won’t work; therefore, there is no sense in buying it.
His argument has some merit; however, sometimes he overelaborates. 



--- SLIDE 39 ---
THE SEMI-COLON (;)
C) Use a semi-colon to seperate items in a series when one or more of the items contains commas:
The judges for the beauty pageant were Mrs. Johnson, a fashion designer; Mrs. Anum, a beauty therapist; Mr Sackeyfio, a trainer; and Dr. Rauf, a lecturer.
The mid-semester examinations will be administered on Monday, July 5; Wednesday, July 7; and Friday, July 9.



--- SLIDE 40 ---
THE SEMI-COLON (;)
D) Use a semi-colon between independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjuction when there are commas within the clauses:
Having studied all night, Kweku entered the exam confidently; but he still found the test items challenging.
In modern time, people can purchase what they need from malls, supermarkets, and discount stores; but in colonial days, when such opportunities did not exist, people relied on general stores and peddlers.

THE COLON (:)
The colon introduces the material which follows it. It may be a list or a statement.
A) Use a colon to separate a list of items from an introductory statement, which often contains the words “as follows”, “the following”, “these”, or “a number”. Note that the list is usually in apposition to some word in the statement:
Ama went to the West Hills Mall and bought the following items: shoes, bags, cosmetics, drinks and cutlery.
The hotel offers four types of accommodation: a single room with bath, private villa, suite and cabin.



--- SLIDE 42 ---
THE COLON (:)
B) Use a colon to separate an introductory statement from an explanation and an appositive:
We hired Nana Ama for two reasons: Her expertise and experience.
Take these words of advice: Never despair.
His adventures had earned him the nickname: Table of men.
C) Use a colon to introduce a long quotation.
Mark Anthony: O’pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth.

The heir apparent’s intentions are clear: He wants to inherit the throne.

THE QUESTION MARK (?)
A question ends with a question mark which separates it from the sentence that follows.
A) Use a question mark after a direct question:
What are your views on campus relationship?
Have you received all your exams scripts?

B) Use a question mark at the end of a question tag;
It is very cold outside the hall, isn’t it?
The students have studied well for the quiz, haven’t they?
NOTE: A question may be in a declarative sentences form. For, example, “You met her on a plane?” The question mark signals the tone in which it would be spoken.

--- SLIDE 78 ---
SKIMMING
It is a fast reading technique that enables us to get the general content or central idea(s) of a text.

We use skimming skill when we want to find out if a certain material is relevant to our area of study or research.

We skim through letters, memoranda, reports, minutes etc to save time.

--- SLIDE 79 ---
SCANNING
It is also a fast reading technique but for a specific piece of information.
It involves searching for a particular item that we are looking for which can take the form of a word, phrase, expression and figures.
The reader has an idea about the items he wishes to discover.
The piece of information sought is usually short or brief.
The reader may not need to read the entire material. 

--- SLIDE 80 ---
CLOSE READING
It is not a fast-reading method but rather a technique used to enable the reader to examine a text thoroughly.
The reader slows down in order to get the full meaning of the material.
The reader looks at all various aspects of the text such as spelling, meaning of words, punctuation, sentence structures, grammatical elements, topic sentence, minor and major supporting ideas and so on.
It helps reader retain the knowledge he has acquired.

--- SLIDE 81 ---
INTENSIVE READING
It enables the reader to acquire complete or deep knowledge of the subject of the reading material.
It shares similar features with the close reading, they complement each other.
It is used to acquire in-depth knowledge of the reading material so that a recall of the information can be done when the need arises.
The reader pays attention to details, organization of the text and the style of writing.
It involves note making in the form of summary or outline. 

--- SLIDE 82 ---
EXTENSIVE READING
It is a fast reading technique which refers to reading widely to broaden the horizon of one’s knowledge.
The reader does not usually use more effort or take pains to capture the details of facts as it is characteristic of intensive reading.
It does not yield a complete absorption of knowledge.
The reader’s concentration depends on the kind of information contained in the text and the reader’s interest level in the information.

--- SLIDE 83 ---
THE SQ3R METHOD
This is a technique for studying as a whole.

It involves various stages that the reader goes through to acquire information which is reserved for future use.

These elements are: Survey (S), Question (Q), Read (R1), Recall (R2), Review (R3).


--- SLIDE 84 ---

	1. First of all look at the title.
         2. Take a look at the date of the first publication.
         3. It is important to skim the introduction of the book.
         4. It is important to survey the content page of the book.

QUESTION (Q)
This is where the reader generates questions that will guide him or her  in his or her reading.
It could be taken from the survey activity.
Some questions might be general or broad while others might be specific.
Questions formulated should be related to the text and elicit the apropriate information.

--- SLIDE 86 ---
READ (R1)
Reading is the central activity in the SQ3R study process.
The ultimate  aim of reading is the acquisition of information and knowledge.
It follows the pre-reading activities of surveying and questioning.
It entails concentration, speed and effective eye movement.
It involves recall which measures its effectivness.

--- SLIDE 87 ---
RECALL (R2)
Recall follows the reading stage, you recollect what you have read.
It can be done at regular intervals during the reading process.
In doing recall, you write the information down or vocalize it.
It is done to ascertain whether the information has really been absorbed.
It is an encouragement to a more effective and intensive reading. 


--- SLIDE 88 ---
REVIEW (R3)
The review follows the recall activity.
The information produced during the recall is examined to find out whether changes are needed.
The reader has the opportunity to pay special atention to important items he could not recall.
Reviewing information constantly makes recall become much easier.

Compound-complex Sentence: It is a sentence that comprises a compound sentence and one or more subordinate clause(s). Eg: After the thief entered the lecture hall because he missed his way, the lecturer stopped speaking and students were dumbfounded.

CONCORD
Concord is the relationship among words in a sentence. It does not only refer to subject verb agreement in a sentence, but also, any relations that exist among different elements in a sentence. It can be seen as different parts of a sentence such as:
Pronoun-antecedent concord occurs when the pronoun and the noun agree in number, gender and person. For instance, Ama loves Kofi because he is intelligent. The pronoun, he has replaced the noun Kofi but in this case, the pronoun, he, is a singular masculine third person pronoun. 
Subject-verb agreement occurs when the verb takes the subject correctly in terms of number and person. In this context, a singular subject agrees with a singular verb while a plural subject agrees with the verb in a plural form. 
Subject-complement concord refers to the agreement between the subject of the sentence and its complement. The complement can be a noun, noun phrase or an adjective. For eg: The man is a prince.      The women are queens.

THE GENERAL OR GOLDEN RULE
The general rule is that, singular subjects take singular verbs and plural subjcts take plural verbs. This is because the subject and the verb must agree in number. The ‘s’ and ‘es’ inflection (ending) makes most nouns plural and most verbs singular. In other words, a regular noun ending in ‘s’ or ‘es’ means it is plural, while a present tense verb ending in ‘s’ or ‘es’ means the verb is singular. For example:
Singular nouns and their inflected plural nouns
Boy (singular)      Boys (plural)
Fox (singular)      Foxes (plural)
Plural verbs and their inflected singular verbs
Go (plural)           Goes (singular)
