 WHAT IS A MODIFIER?
A modifier is a word or a group of words that describes (adds meaning to) another word or group of words in a sentence.
A modifier is a word, phrase or clause that clarifies or describes another word, phrase or clause. Sometimes writers use modifiers incorrectly, leading to strange and unintentionally humorous sentences. The two common types of modifier errors are called misplaced modifiers and dangling modifiers. 
Functions of modifiers
A modifier may identify or describe a headword. For example, 
The woman in the green coat is my aunt.
The hat that you just bought is nice.
The boy with the black hat looks innocent. 
The lady with the Brazilian hair looks beautiful.
The man in the blue shirt is a prisoner.
A modifier may locate. For example, 
The book on the table
The man under the tree
A modifier sets in time or indicates time. For example,
They arrived at 9am.
The flower blossoms in the morning. 
A modifier tells how something happened. For example, 
John played the piano like a drunken Gorilla.
Juliet combs her hair with great care. 
POSITIONS OF MODIFIERS IN A SENTENCE
As a general rule a modifier should come after the word it modifiers. For example, 
The pizza that you just ordered will be ready in the next thirty minutes.
The seats in the back of the theatre are ours.
Occasionally, a modifier is best located directly before the word it modifies. For example,
Walking down the street, Darius saw an automobile. 
Happy and excited, the children played hide and seek.
Bothered by the hard rain, the gardeners quit working for the day. 
Sick and weak, the old man narrated the story. 
MISPLACED MODIFIERS
A misplaced modifier is a modifier that is placed too far from the word or words it modifies. Modifiers that modify the wrong word or seem to modify more than one word in a sentence are called misplaced modifiers.
Misplaced modifiers are words that because of awkward placement, do not describe the words the writer wants to describe. Misplaced modifiers often obscure the meaning of a sentence. To avoid misplaced modifiers, (modifiers) place words as close as possible to (words) what they describe. 
First, underline the modifying phrase and circle the word it modifies. Then, rewrite the sentence so that the modifying phrase is as close as possible to the word it describes.

DANGLING MODIFIERS
A dangling modifier is a word, phrase, or clause that describes something that has been left out of the sentence. When there is nothing that the word, phrase, or clause can modify, the modifier is said to dangle.
The dangling modifiers are usually introductory phrases that do not go with the subjects of the sentences. These phrases are called dangling modifiers because they have nothing to modify. Dangling modifiers do not logically seem to modify any word in a sentence.
A modifier that opens a sentence must be followed immediately by the word it is meant to describe; otherwise, the modifier is said to be dangling, and the sentence takes on unintended meaning.
A modifying phrase or clause is said to dangle when it has no stated word to describe. To correct a dangling modifier, you must rewrite the sentence and supply the word that is to be modified.
Almost all dangling modifiers begin sentences.  
Almost all dangling modifiers are phrases beginning with an –ing ending word (gerund) or with an –ed ending word (past participle).
If an introductory phrase doesn’t modify the subject of the sentence, it is a dangling modifier.
Tense is very important while dealing with dangling modifiers. Tense in A must agree with tense in B.
SENTENCE ADVERBIALS (Are not dangling) – They comment on the main sentence and link it with other sentences.

AMBIGUITY
Definition of AMBIGUITY
Ambiguity is a term that refers to a situation where a word or a group of words (phrase, clause or sentence) has more than one meaning. The term ambiguity is used to describe a word, a phrase or a sentence with a multiple meanings. We talk of ambiguity when a sentence is opened to more than one interpretation. This becomes obstacle to effective communication - whether oral or written.
Forms of Ambiguity
Ambiguity can occur in two forms.
 It can be:
Planned or intentional ambiguity: This is done consciously and deliberately by the speaker to achieve some effect. Intentional/planned is carefully constructed by the reader or writer for a specific effect. It is employed in literature (poems, drama, songs etc) or in comedy.
				OR 
Unplanned or unintentional ambiguity: Unplanned or unintentional ambiguity is one that occurs in speech or in writing without the awareness of the language users. This brings about miss-communication. In this form, the speaker is not aware and does not intentionally make the ambiguous statement. This must be avoided, especially in academic writing
TYPES OF AMBIGUITY
There are three main types of ambiguity. There are;
Every language is structured at various levels, such as lexical, phonological, syntactic morphological, and discourse levels. Ambiguous information can occur at any given point in a sentence. There are two types of ambiguity syntactic and semantic ambiguities.

Phonological Ambiguity
It refers to how words are pronounced. This type of ambiguity deals with when the sounds of words are being used. It occurs usually when words with similar sounds are pronounced. Two quite different words might sound the same, whether spelt or not. It may be triggered by stress and intonation when pronouncing words.
Words like:
Sail-sale-sell, I scream – Ice cream, cease – seize, Ice – Eyes, Cut - Cat	, Hut – Hat, Must - Mast		
Thus, when one fails to articulate the sounds properly, one’s speech is likely to be ambiguous. Therefore, good pronunciation, intonation, stress, and word breaks are the tools for disambiguating phonological ambiguity.

Semantic ambiguity comes about as a result of some words that have more than one meaning. Such words when used in a sentence can cause the sentence to be ambiguous. This type of ambiguity refers to when a word has more than one meaning. Semantic ambiguity refers to difference in meaning. There are two types of semantic ambiguity. Some words sound and written the same but they are not identical in meaning, it means whether they are two words (homonyms), or one word used in two different ways (polysemy).

Squinting Modifiers as a source of ambiguity
These modifiers are placed in such a way that they can be interpreted as modifying what presides or proceed it.
For example, 
Students who read occasionally sleep off in class. 
“Occasionally” is being described here as a squint modifier because it is placed between read and sleep off. It can, thus, be interpreted as modifying any of them. Squint modifiers are thus very similar to misplaced restrictors. Squint modifiers are however different because of the following:
They are usually time or frequency adverbs such as – yesterday, occasionally, usually, always, frequently; manner adverbs such as slowly.
They are usually placed between two verbs – one of which is in a relative clause and the other in a main clause. For example, “read” and “sleep off”.
Squint modifiers can be corrected by 
Removing the modifier from the middle position and placing it close to only one of the verbs.
For exapme, Students who occasionally read sleep off in class. (They read occasionally).
Students who read sleep off occasionally in class.

In (b), yesterday modifies “questioned”.
