VECTORS AND SCALARS AND COMPONENTS OF A VECTOR 
         
All physical quantities can be grouped into scalars and vectors. In Physics, scalars and vectors are 
used to explain concepts. Mass, distance and speed are examples of scalars. Displacement, 
velocity, acceleration and force are examples of vectors. Also, this session teaches you how to 
break a given vector into its components. A vector lying in an x-y plane and directed at an angle 
has both x-component and y-component. How to determine magnitude and direction of vectors are 
also discussed. This session introduces you to another method of adding two vectors. The 
parallelogram law of vectors is used to find the resultants of two vectors. This law can be applied 
graphically or by calculation to find the resultant of two vectors.

Vectors and Scalars 
In Physics, physical quantities are grouped into scalars and vectors. Not all physical quantities 
involve a direction. 
 A scalar quantity is specified by a single value with an appropriate unit and has no direction. That 
is, a scalar quantity has only magnitude but no direction. Physical quantities like temperature, 
volume, mass, time intervals, pressure and energy are examples of scalars because they have only 
magnitudes with unit.  
A vector quantity has magnitude as well as direction. It is typically represented by an arrow whose 
direction is the same as that of the quantity and whose length is proportional to the quantity’s 
magnitude. The direction of a vector is often expressed as an anticlockwise angle of rotation of the 
vector about its "tail" (starting point) from due East. Using this convention, a vector E with a 
direction of 40 degrees is a vector that has been rotated 40 degrees in an anticlockwise direction 
relative to due east as shown in Figure 1.1. Examples of vector quantities are displacement, 
velocity and acceleration.

Components of a Vector 
A vector that starts from the origin and is directed along the customary axes (ie. x, y, and z) will 
have only one component. For example, if a vector D moves 2 units along the x-axis, its 
components will read as D (2,0) which means that it has only x-component.  
 
In situations where a vector is directed at angles to the customary coordinate axes, a useful method 
will be employed to transform the vector into two parts with each part being directed along the 
coordinate axes. For example, a vector that is directed northwest can be thought of as having two 
parts (ie. a northward part and a westward part). A vector with coordinate B (3,4) will have 3 units 
along the x-axis and 4 units along the y-axis.

Laws of Vector Algebra 
If A, B and C are vectors and p and q are scalars, then 
1. A + B = B + A                                     Commutative Law for Addition 
2. A + (B +C) = (A + B) + C                   Associative Law for Addition 
3. p(qA) = (pq)A = q(pA)                        Associative Law of Multiplication 
4. (p + q )A = pA + qA                            Distributive Law 
5. p(A + B) = pA + pB                             Distributive Law 
 
Note that in these Laws only multiplication of a vector by one or more scalars is defined.

Vector Algebra 
A vector is represented analytically by a bold-faced letter such as A. Geometrically it is represented 
by an arrow PQ where P is called the initial point and Q is called the terminal point. The magnitude 
or length of the vector is then denoted by |𝐴|. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Figure 1.7: (a) A position vector A between two points P and Q.  (b) Two vectors A and B having 
same magnitude and same direction (c) The vectors A and -A have the same magnitude but are in 
opposite direction.  
 
The operations of addition, subtraction and multiplication familiar in the algebra of real numbers 
are with suitable definitions capable of extension to an algebra of vectors. 
The following definitions are fundamental. 
1. Two vectors A and B are equal if they have the same magnitude and direction regardless 
of their initial points. Thus A = B in figure 1.7b. 
2. A vector having direction opposite to that of vector A but with the same length is denoted 
by –A as in figure 1.7c. 
3. The sum or resultant of vectors A and B of figure 1.8a is a vector C formed by placing the 
initial point of B on the terminal point of A and joining the initial point of A to the terminal 
point of B [see figure 1.8b]. We write C = A + B. This definition is equivalent to the 
parallelogram law for vector addition as indicated in figure 1.8c.
